# 01 -- Mirror magic and fold-cut symmetry

> **The idea.** Some shapes have a secret fold line where one half matches the
> other half exactly. That fold line is called a *line of symmetry*. Today we go
> find it and make it.

**You need:** a small flat mirror (a phone-sized makeup mirror is perfect),
paper, pencil, safe scissors, some old magazines or printed pictures (optional).
**Time:** 20 to 30 minutes.

---

## Game 1: the mirror that finishes the picture

1. Draw half of something simple: half a heart, half a butterfly, half a face,
   down the middle.
2. Stand the mirror straight up along the middle line.
3. Look into the mirror. The whole shape appears, completed by the reflection.

**Ask her:** can you draw half a letter so the mirror makes a whole letter? Try
**A**, **M**, **T**, **W**. Now try **R** or **F**. What goes wrong? (Some
letters do not have a matching half. Those letters are *not* symmetric.)

Make a list together: which capital letters can the mirror complete, and which
cannot?

## Game 2: fold and cut

1. Fold a piece of paper in half.
2. Cut a shape starting and ending on the folded edge (do not cut the fold all
   the way off).
3. Open it up.

The shape is the same on both sides of the fold. That fold is its line of
symmetry. Make a heart this way (cut half a heart against the fold). Make a tree,
a person, a vase.

**Ask her:** before you open it, can you guess what the whole shape will look
like?

## Game 3: the snowflake (this one connects to Ben)

1. Start with a square or a circle of paper.
2. Fold it in half, then in half again, then once more on the slant, so you have
   a skinny wedge.
3. Snip little shapes out of the folded edges.
4. Open it slowly.

You get a snowflake with the same pattern repeating around and around. A real
snowflake has **six** matching sides. Ben can build a perfect six-sided shape (a
hexagon) with a compass; this is the same six showing up in a different way.

**Ask her:** how many matching parts does your snowflake have? Count the points.

---

## Notice together (no pressure)

- A line of symmetry is a fold where both halves land exactly on each other.
- Some shapes have one line of symmetry (a heart). Some have many (a square has
  four, a circle has endless). **Ask her:** how many fold lines can she find on a
  square piece of paper by folding it different ways?
- Symmetry is everywhere once you look: faces, butterflies, leaves, letters,
  buildings. Go on a five-minute symmetry hunt around the room.

## For the grown-up

- The whole concept is "fold so the halves match." Keep that physical. If she can
  fold a shape so the two halves cover each other, it is symmetric; if she cannot,
  it is not.
- The square-has-four-lines discovery (two through the middle of the sides, two
  through the corners) is a great one to let her find by folding, not by being
  told.
- Try Mathigon Polypad's mirror and symmetry tools (see `../resources.md`) for a
  no-scissors digital version on a rainy day.
