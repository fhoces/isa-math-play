02 -- Pattern blocks and tangrams

The idea. Shapes fit together. You can build big shapes out of small ones, fill an outline more than one way, and make pictures out of a few simple pieces. This is your first taste of how shapes tile and combine, the same thing Ben studies as "tiling."

You need: pattern blocks if you have them (the classic set: yellow hexagon, red trapezoid, blue rhombus, green triangle, orange square, tan thin rhombus). No blocks? Use the tangram below, or do this on Mathigon Polypad (free, see ../resources.md), which has both sets on screen. Time: 20 to 30 minutes.

With pattern blocks

Fill the hexagon

Take one yellow hexagon. Now cover it exactly using other pieces.

  • How many green triangles fill one hexagon? (Find out: it is 6.)
  • How many blue rhombi? How many red trapezoids?
  • Can you fill it using a mix, like one trapezoid and the rest triangles?

Ask her: how many different ways can you fill the same hexagon? Keep a tally. There are more than you would think.

Build out, not just in

  • Make a bigger hexagon out of smaller pieces.
  • Cover the whole table edge to edge with hexagons and triangles, no gaps, no overlaps. That "no gaps" filling is called a tiling. Notice that hexagons and triangles are happy to do it but circles would leave gaps.
  • Make a picture: a flower, a turtle, a rocket. Pattern blocks are great for this.

Ask her: which shapes leave gaps when you try to tile with just them? (Try all-pentagons. They will not close up. That is a real and surprising fact.)

With a tangram (no special blocks needed)

A tangram is one square cut into 7 pieces: five triangles (two big, one medium, two small), one square, one parallelogram. Cut a square along these lines to make your own (ask for a print-ready template and I will make one):

   +------------------+
   |\        /|      /|
   | \      / |     / |
   |  \    /  |    /  |
   |   \  /   |   /   |
   |    \/    |  /    |
   |    /\    | /     |
   |   /  \   |/      |
   |  /    \  +-------+
   | /      \ |      /|
   |/        \|     / |
   +----------+----+--+

(The exact cut lines are fiddly; ask me for a clean printable version.)

The one rule: every puzzle uses all seven pieces, flat, no overlaps.

  • Put the 7 pieces back into the original square. (Harder than it sounds.)
  • Make a cat, a person running, a boat, a house, a bird. Look up "tangram silhouettes" for outlines to match.

Ask her: can you make two different pictures that each use all seven pieces?

Notice together (no pressure)

  • Small shapes build big shapes, and big shapes break into small ones. Six green triangles are a hexagon.
  • Some shapes tile (fill space with no gaps) and some do not. Triangles, squares, and hexagons tile. Regular pentagons do not. Ben proves why only certain shapes tile; you get to discover that they do.
  • Three green triangles already make the trapezoid; two trapezoids make the hexagon. There are little number patterns hiding in the blocks.

For the grown-up

  • The richest question is "how many different ways can you fill the hexagon." Counting the ways is real combinatorial thinking dressed up as play.
  • The "pentagons leave gaps" discovery is worth setting up on purpose. It is genuinely surprising and it is the exact question Ben's tiling exploration will answer with angles (a regular pentagon's corner is 108 degrees, which does not divide 360 evenly, so they cannot close around a point). You do not need to say any of that to Isa; just let her find that they will not fit.
  • Mathigon Polypad has both pattern blocks and tangrams as drag-and-drop tools, which is great when you do not have the physical set.
