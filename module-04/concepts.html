04 -- Number shapes you can build

The idea. Some numbers have a shape. You can build them out of dots, coins, or blocks. Squares make squares, and a special set of numbers makes perfect triangles. Today we build the numbers and find a surprise hiding inside them.

You need: about 30 small same-size things to lay out (coins, buttons, dried beans, or small blocks), paper and pencil. Optional: two colors of counters for the last game. No counters? Draw dots. Time: 20 to 30 minutes.

Game 1: square numbers

  1. Lay out 1 coin. That is a tiny 1 by 1 square.
  2. Now make a 2 by 2 square. Count: 4.
  3. Now 3 by 3. Count: 9. Then 4 by 4. Count: 16.

These are the square numbers: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. Each one is a full square with the same number of dots on every side.

Ask her: how many coins for a 5 by 5 square? Build it and check. (It is 25.)

Ask her: is 12 a square number? Try to make a square out of 12. (You cannot: it makes a 3 by 4 rectangle, not a square. Only special numbers are square.)

Game 2: triangle numbers

  1. Put down 1 dot.
  2. Add a row of 2 under it. Now you have 3, in a little triangle.
  3. Add a row of 3 under that. Now 6.
  4. Add a row of 4. Now 10.

Each time you add one longer row. These are the triangle numbers: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. They are the rows 1, then 1+2, then 1+2+3, then 1+2+3+4.

Ask her: what comes after 10? Add a row of 5. (You get 15.)

This is the same growing staircase she met in Module 03, now stacked into a shape.

Game 3: two triangles make a rectangle (the surprise)

  1. Build the triangle number 6 out of one color: rows of 1, 2, 3.
  2. Build a second 6 in another color, the same shape.
  3. Turn one of them upside down and slide the two triangles together.

They lock into a rectangle! For the triangle made of rows 1, 2, 3 the rectangle is 3 by 4, which is 12. And 12 is two sixes.

Ask her: so what is half of that 3 by 4 rectangle? (One triangle: 6.) That is why a triangle number is "half a rectangle."

Try it again with the triangle 10 (rows 1, 2, 3, 4). Two of them make a 4 by 5 rectangle, which is 20. Half of 20 is 10. It always works.

Bonus noticing: squares grow by odd numbers

Look back at your squares. Going 1, 4, 9, 16, how much did you add each time?

  • 1 to 4: added 3
  • 4 to 9: added 5
  • 9 to 16: added 7

Always the next odd number. Each new square is the old one plus an L-shaped layer wrapped around two sides, and that L is the next odd number of dots.

Ask her: how many dots in the next L, to go from 16 to 25? (9, the next odd.)

Notice together (no pressure)

  • Numbers can have shapes. 4, 9, 16 are squares; 3, 6, 10 are triangles.
  • Two copies of the same triangle number always fit into a rectangle, so a triangle number is exactly half of that rectangle.
  • Square numbers grow by the odd numbers 3, 5, 7, 9, one L-layer at a time.

For the grown-up

  • The richest moment is Game 3. Do not tell her the two triangles make a rectangle: hand her two paper triangles (or two colors of counters) and let her discover it by fitting them together. The click of recognition is the whole point.
  • The general fact: two copies of the n-th triangle number make an n by (n+1) rectangle, so the triangle number is n times (n+1), divided by 2. She does not need the formula; the picture is the proof.
